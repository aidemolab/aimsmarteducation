Listing every outcome: hold one choice fixed

Goal: List all possible outcomes by holding one choice fixed at a time.

In this video you'll learn one thing.

How to list every outcome, so that none is missing.

Here's a puzzle.

A café's lunch deal is one sandwich and one drink.

The sandwiches are cheese, ham and egg.

The drinks are water and juice.

The sign shows five lunches.

Is the sign complete? Lock in one card: five, six or nine.

Hold on to your guess.

Exam questions often say "list all the possible outcomes", so you need a method you can trust.

In N4 you found the factor pairs of 24.

You tried 1, then 2, then 3, then 4, in order, so you could not skip a pair.

Today we use the same habit.

We step off our number scale for a moment, because listing is about structure, not position on a line.

So we use a grid.

First, we give each sandwich its own row.

One row for each sandwich means no sandwich can be forgotten.

Next, we hold cheese fixed.

Keeping cheese still means only the drink changes, so the row is easy to finish.

Cheese with water, then cheese with juice.

The row is full, because there are only two drinks.

We write the drinks in the same order in every row, so a gap would show at once.

Pause point. Pause the video now. Predict the ham row. Lock in one card, then press play.

We hold ham fixed: ham with water, then ham with juice.

Then we hold egg fixed: egg with water, then egg with juice.

Let's slow down here, because this is the key step.

Every sandwich has had its turn, and every row has both of its slots filled.

Equal, full rows tell us that nothing is missing.

No two slots hold the same lunch, so nothing is counted twice.

Two, plus two, plus two, makes six lunches.

Now back to the puzzle.

The sign's five cards fill five slots, and the egg and juice slot is empty.

So the sign was not complete: there are six lunches.

Its random order hid the gap; the grid showed it.

Using the digits 1, 2 and 3, each once, list all the three-digit numbers you can make.

We hold the first digit fixed, starting with 1, so the rows go in order.

With 1 in front, the digits 2 and 3 are left.

They can go in two orders: 123, then 132.

Both count, because 123 and 132 are different numbers.

Pause point. Pause the video now. In every row we write the smaller leftover digit first. Why? Lock in one card, then press play.

Every row follows the same pattern, so if a number were missing, its gap would stand out.

With 2 in front, we get 213, then 231.

With 3 in front, we get 312, then 321.

Every digit has had its turn at the front, and every row has two numbers.

Two, plus two, plus two, makes six numbers, with none missing and none repeated.

You've learned to list all possible outcomes by holding one choice fixed at a time.

Give each first choice its own row.

Hold it fixed, and change the other choice in the same order until the row is full.

Equal, full rows show that nothing is missing.

And each full row moves our pin along the number scale by the same jump: two, four, six.
