Back to one: the reciprocal

In this video you'll learn one thing.

You'll learn how to find the reciprocal of any number that isn't zero.

Here's a puzzle.

In N2, you found that 6 ÷ 1/3 is 18.

And 6 × 3 is also 18.

Dividing by one third gave the same answer as multiplying by 3.

Is that luck, or is there a reason? Hold on to your guess.

The reason is a number called the reciprocal.

You'll meet reciprocals inside calculations and in formulas, so it pays to find them quickly.

You already know that four quarters make one whole.

You know how to multiply fractions, from N2.9.

You know how to change a mixed number into an improper fraction.

And from N2.7, you know that a negative times a negative is positive.

Here's a new word.

The reciprocal of a number is the number you multiply it by to get exactly 1.

Let's start with 4.

What do we multiply 4 by to land on 1?

We multiply by 1/4, because 4 lots of one quarter make one whole.

So the reciprocal of 4 is 1/4.

You can always find a reciprocal by working out 1 divided by the number.

That's because "what times 4 makes 1?" is undone by dividing 1 by 4, just like in N3.2.

For a fraction, the reciprocal is the fraction flipped upside down.

The reciprocal of 2/3 is 3/2.

That works because 2/3 × 3/2 is 6/6, which is 1.

For a decimal, write it as a fraction first, because then you can flip it.

0.5 is 1/2, so its reciprocal is 2.

Check: 0.5 × 2 is 1.

Now a trap.

Pause point. Pause the video now. What is the reciprocal of −5? Lock in one card: 5, −5, 1/5 or −1/5. Then press play.

Let's slow down here, because this is the key step.

We test each card by multiplying it by −5.

−5 × 5 is −25, so 5 is not the reciprocal.

−5 × −5 is 25, and −5 × 1/5 is −1, so those fail too.

−5 × −1/5 is positive 1, because a negative times a negative is positive.

So the reciprocal of −5 is −1/5.

A reciprocal keeps the same sign as the number. It is not the negative of the number.

What about zero?

Zero times any number is zero.

So multiplying zero can never reach 1.

That's why zero has no reciprocal, and why our goal says a non-zero number.

Now we can solve the puzzle.

1/3 × 3 is 1, so 3 is the reciprocal of 1/3.

Dividing by 1/3 asks how many thirds fit into 6.

Each whole holds 3 thirds, so 6 wholes hold 6 × 3 thirds, which is 18.

So dividing by a number gives the same answer as multiplying by its reciprocal.

It wasn't luck.

Let's find the reciprocal of 1 3/5.

Pause point. Pause the video now. Can we just flip the 3/5 part, or must we write 1 3/5 as one fraction first? Choose, and justify your choice. Then press play.

Step one: we write it as one fraction first.

That's because the reciprocal has to multiply the whole number to make 1, not just its fraction part.

One whole is 5 fifths, plus 3 fifths makes 8 fifths.

So 1 3/5 is 8/5.

Step two: flip the fraction.

The reciprocal of 8/5 is 5/8.

Step three: check, by multiplying back.

8/5 × 5/8 is 40/40, which is 1.

So the reciprocal of 1 3/5 is 5/8.

Flipping only the fraction part gives 1 5/3.

But 1 5/3 is 8/3, and 8/5 × 8/3 is 64/15, which is not 1.

So the check shows it's wrong.

You've learned to find the reciprocal of a non-zero number.

It's the number you multiply by to get 1, so it's 1 divided by the number.

For a fraction, flip it.

For a decimal or a mixed number, write it as one fraction first.

The sign stays the same, and zero has no reciprocal.
